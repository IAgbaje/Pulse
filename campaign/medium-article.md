# Your Salary Hasn't Moved Since 2023. The Naira Has.

*A data-backed look at what actually happened to Nigerian tech compensation between 2023 and 2026 — and why the nominal numbers are hiding a real pay cut.*

---

**The headline number sounds fine. It isn't.**

If you've been in Nigerian tech for the last three years and your salary hasn't changed, here's what you should know: the median monthly gross salary in this industry moved from ₦581,000 in 2023 to ₦600,000 in 2026. That's a 3% nominal increase. Three percent over three years.

In the same period, the naira went from approximately ₦450 to the dollar to over ₦1,580 to the dollar.

Do the math. The median Nigerian tech salary in 2023 was worth roughly $1,290 a month. Today, ₦600,000 buys you about $380. That's not stagnation. That's a 70% collapse in purchasing power measured against any hard currency — and a significant real wage cut even measured entirely in naira, where headline inflation ran over 30% in 2024 alone.

This is the story that Pulse — a new anonymous Nigerian tech salary index — is trying to make legible with data.

---

## Where the data comes from

Pulse combines two datasets: a 2023 community survey of Nigerian tech professionals curated by Lola Soleye (87 records, skewed toward Product Management), and 48 live anonymous submissions collected in 2026 via the Pulse form. 135 records total. Not a large sample — but large enough to see the shape of the problem, and growing.

The numbers I'm about to cite are real, verified, and conservative. Every stat on Pulse requires a minimum of 5 records before it's displayed. Narrow segments are flagged. Nothing is extrapolated or estimated. If a number is on the site, there's a real salary behind it.

---

## What ₦600K actually buys you

The 2026 median of ₦600,000 monthly gross is, in isolation, a large-sounding number. It isn't. Here's what it means in context:

**In dollar terms:** At the current parallel-market rate, ₦600K ≈ $380/month, or roughly $4,560/year. A junior developer at a mid-sized US company earns that in a week.

**Relative to the cost of living:** Nigerian inflation hit 34.8% year-on-year in December 2023 and stayed elevated through 2025. A salary that was worth ₦581K in 2023 purchasing power needs to be worth roughly ₦850K-900K today to have kept pace with inflation. The average Nigerian tech worker is earning ₦600K.

**Against the diaspora:** Pulse captures a small number of diaspora and remote workers paid in foreign currency — engineers in the UK earning GBP 4,000-6,000/month, remote workers earning $3,000-5,000/month in USD. The divergence between onshore and offshore comp is the starkest number in the dataset. Same work, sometimes the same employer. Three to five times the monthly pay.

## The negotiation data is the most actionable thing here

Here's something the dataset reveals that doesn't get talked about enough: negotiating your salary produces a measurable, statistically significant premium.

In the 2026 data, professionals who negotiated their last compensation package earn a median of roughly 13% more than those who didn't. That's not a small difference. On a ₦600K baseline, 13% is ₦78,000 a month — ₦936,000 annually. That's real money, compounding from the moment you accept the offer.

And yet fewer than half of respondents in the dataset negotiated.

The most common reason people don't negotiate isn't ignorance. It's that they don't know what the market actually pays, so they can't make the case confidently. "I know the market pays X and I want Y" is a different conversation than "I'd love a bit more if possible." The first one works.

This is exactly what Pulse exists to enable.

---

## The seniority ladder still works — but the rungs have moved

One more data point worth sitting with: the median salary for a Senior engineer (4-8 years experience) in the dataset is roughly 2-3× the Junior (0-2 years) median. Experience still produces a meaningful pay premium in Nigerian tech. The multiplier is real.

What's changed is the floor. Junior roles in 2023 that paid ₦200-300K gross are now offering ₦300-400K nominally — but that "increase" doesn't account for the naira's collapse. The floor in real terms has dropped.

The practical implication: the single highest-ROI action for anyone in Nigerian tech under 5 years of experience right now is not a lateral move for a marginal salary bump. It's either a vertical move (same employer, next level, which the data shows is the biggest single comp jump available) or a move to a remote role paid in foreign currency.

## Why this kind of data doesn't exist yet — and why it needs to

Nigerian salaries are opaque by design. There are cultural reasons (money is private, comparison feels like competition), institutional reasons (HR teams don't want you to know what your colleague earns), and structural reasons (there's no equivalent of Glassdoor that's actually used, no Bureau of Labor Statistics equivalent, no sectoral pay surveys).

The result is that every salary negotiation in Nigerian tech happens with one party — the employer — holding most of the information and the other party — you — negotiating in the dark.

Pulse is a small attempt to fix that asymmetry. It's one person's project, not a research institution. The sample size is real but limited. The form is anonymous, four minutes long, and doesn't ask for your name, email, company, or job title. You get to contribute a data point to a public index and check your own numbers against it immediately.

Every salary shared makes the next negotiation sharper. That's the entire premise.

---

## What the data still needs

The 2026 Pulse dataset skews heavily toward fintech (the biggest industry representation) and toward mid-level roles. Engineering is the most represented function. Product, Design, Data, and especially non-Lagos locations are underrepresented.

This matters because the headline median of ₦600K describes a fintech-heavy, Lagos-heavy, engineering-heavy population. If you work in health tech in Abuja as a designer, the ₦600K number is directionally useful but not specifically yours.

The index improves every time someone contributes a record. Right now, the dataset most needs:

- **Data & Analytics** professionals (heavily underrepresented)
- **Product Design / UX** (only a handful of records)
- **Legal, Ops, and Finance** functions in tech companies
- **Non-Lagos locations** — Abuja, Port Harcourt, and remote-Nigeria
- **Non-fintech industries** — healthtech, edtech, logistics tech, SaaS

If you work in any of these, your submission doesn't just help you — it creates a reference point for the next person in your position who's trying to negotiate without one.
